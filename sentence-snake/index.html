Sentence Snake

A small language model plays Snake without ever seeing the board. Before each move the game writes one plain English sentence for each of the three moves (straight, left, right). The model reads each one, answers a single question with a probability, and the snake takes the highest. It all runs on a laptop with no network.

    How a move gets decided

    1. The game looks at the three moves and writes a sentence for each: does it crash, eat, get closer or farther, and how many open cells the snake could still reach.
    2. The model reads the question and one sentence together and returns one number between 0 and 1.
    3. The snake takes the move with the highest number. There is no search, no lookahead and no safety override in code.

    The model is ModernBERT-base, an open encoder that reads text, with a small scoring head on top: 149.2 million parameters in all, of which 199 thousand are the head we added. It learned by copying a teacher. A hand-written rule (never crash, never pick a move that leaves less room than the snake is long, otherwise get closer to the food) played 150 practice games with 10% random moves mixed in, so the model also saw bad positions. Every move the rule took was labelled 1 and the moves it passed over were labelled 0. Training ran 800 steps in under eight minutes on the laptop's integrated Intel GPU.

    Its probabilities are calibrated. On held-out moves where it said 30 to 40%, it averaged 35.5% and the rule took the move 34.3% of the time (753 moves). Its average calibration error across all held-out moves is 0.0085.

    The move that ended game 1007

    On this game the model and its teacher agreed on every move but two. The last disagreement killed it. Here is what it read:

      Neither real option fits the question. One gets closer to the food but walls the snake in. The other is safe but moves away. So both score low, and the gap between them (4.6% against 3.6%) is too small to mean anything. The rule has a hard penalty for boxing itself in. The model only has a question that asks for two things at once, and it rated each move on its own without comparing it with the other two.

      This is the lesson from the lab's Tetris model. A yes/no score for each option is fine for judging one thing on its own, but it ranks poorly when no option is a clean yes. The fix in Tetris was a head trained to compare all the options of a turn against each other, and with it the Tetris model matched its teacher in 2 of 3 test games. Snake has not had that fix yet.
